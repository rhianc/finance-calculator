How the wealth comparison works

The chart is this arithmetic, once a month. Rates you type are effective annual rates. Home value and both portfolios compound monthly. Rent and the operating costs step up on each anniversary. Dollars on the chart are then divided by inflation.

Loan

\(P\) is the amount borrowed, \(R\) the annual mortgage rate, \(r = R/12\), and \(N\) the number of payments.

\[ M = \begin{cases} P / N & r = 0 \\ P \dfrac{r(1+r)^{N}}{(1+r)^{N}-1} & r > 0 \end{cases} \]

Balance after \(t\) payments, before the extra cents of a last-payment cleanup:

\[ B_t = \begin{cases} P\left(1 - t/N\right) & r = 0 \\ P(1+r)^{t} - M \dfrac{(1+r)^{t}-1}{r} & r > 0 \end{cases} \]

The two portfolios

\(H_t\) is the home value and \(g\) is appreciation. \(D\) is the down payment. \(c_b\) is buyer closing costs and \(c_s\) is the cost to sell, each as a fraction of price. \(O_t\) is that month's housing cash: principal, interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, HOA, and PMI. \(\tau_t\) is the tax saved that month. \(R_t\) is rent. \(r_s\) is the monthly stock return after the drag, \(r_s = (1+s)^{1/12}-1\).

\[ H_t = H_0 (1+g)^{t/12} \]
\[ S_0 = D + c_b H_0 \]
\[ S_t = S_{t-1}(1+r_s) + (O_t - R_t) \]
\[ T_0 = 0,\quad T_t = T_{t-1}(1+r_s) + \tau_t \]

\(S\) is the renter's portfolio. \(T\) is the owner's tax savings, invested at the same return. Because those savings sit in \(T\), they are not also subtracted from the renter's deposit.

If you sold that month

\[ \mathrm{Gain}_t = \max\left(0,\ H_t(1-c_s) - H_0(1+c_b)\right) \]
\[ G_t = \max\left(0,\ \mathrm{Gain}_t - X_t\right) \times (\text{federal gain rate} + \text{state rate}) \]

\(X_t\) is 0 until the home has been held 24 months, then $250,000 for single filers and $500,000 for a joint return. Before 12 months the federal piece uses your ordinary federal rate. After 12 months it uses the long-term rate you entered.

\[ L_t = H_t(1-c_s) - B_t - G_t \]
\[ W^H_t = \frac{L_t + T_t}{(1+i)^{t/12}},\qquad W^R_t = \frac{S_t}{(1+i)^{t/12}} \]

\(i\) is inflation. \(W^H\) is the buy line and \(W^R\) is the rent line, both in today's dollars. \(L_t\) is what's left after the loan, the sale, and tax on the gain.

Tax saved

Interest is deductible on the portion of the balance under the $750,000 acquisition-debt cap. At the start of month \(t\):

\[ \text{Deductible interest}_t = \text{Interest}_t \times \min\left(1,\ \frac{750{,}000}{B_{t-1}}\right) \]

Property tax and the state income tax you enter share the 2026 SALT cap of $40,400. Above $505,000 of MAGI the cap shrinks by 30 cents per dollar and does not fall below $10,000. The federal benefit is only the extra itemizing the house creates:

\[ \tau^{\mathrm{fed}} = \left[\max(0,\ I_{\mathrm{own}} - \mathrm{Std}) - \max(0,\ I_{\mathrm{rent}} - \mathrm{Std})\right] \times \text{federal rate} \]

\(I_{\mathrm{own}}\) includes deductible interest and the property tax that still fits under the cap. \(I_{\mathrm{rent}}\) is your other itemized deductions only. \(\mathrm{Std}\) is $16,100 or $32,200. The year's federal savings are recognized month by month as the running total crosses the standard deduction, so early months in the year can show $0 federally. State savings each month are deductible interest times your state rate.

Property tax over time

\(\rho\) is the rate you entered. In California mode the assessed value grows with inflation, and never by more than 2% a year. In market mode the bill tracks appreciation, so the effective rate stays constant.

\[ \mathrm{Tax}^{\mathrm{CA}}_y = H_0 \,(1+\min(i,\ 0.02))^y \,\rho \]
\[ \mathrm{Tax}^{\mathrm{market}}_y = H_0 \,(1+g)^y \,\rho \]

Where the payment formula comes from

Each month the balance grows by \(r\), then the payment is subtracted. After \(t\) months the unpaid balance is the loan grown forward, minus the future value of the payments already made:

\[ B_t = P(1+r)^t - M \sum_{k=0}^{t-1} (1+r)^k \]

The sum is a geometric series, \(\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}(1+r)^k = ((1+r)^t - 1)/r\). The loan is paid off when \(B_N = 0\). Solving that for \(M\) gives the payment formula above. The chart is that payment on a $1,000 loan.

Left out on purpose

  • Extra principal payments, refinancing, and adjustable rates.
  • Deducting PMI, and FHA loans that keep a premium for the life of the loan.
  • The 3.8% net investment income tax, unless you fold it into the long-term gains rate.
  • A partial home-sale exclusion, depreciation from a rental period, and improvements added to basis.
  • The SALT cap's return to $10,000 in 2030. Entered MAGI does phase the cap down today.
  • Lumpy repairs, special assessments, and the chance that returns bounce around instead of arriving in a straight line.
