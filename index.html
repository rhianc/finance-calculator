Tax rules
$/yr

The income above is wages, and it is also the MAGI used for the SALT cap. Federal tax uses the 2026 ordinary brackets, not one flat rate. A dollar of housing deduction saves whatever bracket it falls in, and only once itemizing beats the standard deduction ($16,100 single, $32,200 joint). Mortgage interest is deductible on up to $750,000 of the loan. Property tax shares the SALT cap with the state and local income tax. That cap is $40,400 in 2026, rises 1% a year through 2029, and returns to $10,000 in 2030. Above $505,000 of income it shrinks by 30 cents per dollar, and it does not fall below $10,000. State tax on the interest assumes the state allows the deduction. The sale is taxed on top of this income: 0%, 15%, or 20% federally, then the 3.8% net investment income tax when income including the gain is over $250,000 joint or $200,000 single, then state tax on the gain.

How the wealth comparison works

The chart is this arithmetic, once a month. The mortgage rate is nominal: its monthly rate is the annual rate divided by 12. Appreciation, the stock return, and inflation are effective annual rates, turned into a monthly rate with \((1+r)^{1/12}-1\). Drag is subtracted from the stock return before that conversion. Rent, insurance, maintenance, HOA, and property tax step up on each anniversary and stay flat through the year. The chart then divides by inflation.

Loan

\(P\) is the amount borrowed, \(R\) the annual mortgage rate, \(r = R/12\), and \(N\) the number of payments.

\[ M = \begin{cases} P / N & r = 0 \\ P \dfrac{r(1+r)^{N}}{(1+r)^{N}-1} & r > 0 \end{cases} \]

Balance after \(t\) payments. A leftover under half a cent is wiped to zero. Unpaid interest is not added to the balance: if the payment does not cover the interest, the balance stays where it is.

\[ B_t = \begin{cases} P\left(1 - t/N\right) & r = 0 \\ P(1+r)^{t} - M \dfrac{(1+r)^{t}-1}{r} & r > 0 \end{cases} \]

The two portfolios

\(H_t\) is the home value and \(g\) is appreciation. \(D\) is the down payment. \(c_b\) is buyer closing costs and \(c_s\) is the cost to sell, each as a fraction of price. \(O_t\) is that month's housing cash: principal, interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, HOA, and PMI. PMI is the original loan times its annual rate, divided by 12, and it is charged only while the balance is above 80% of the purchase price. \(\tau_t\) is the tax saved that month. \(R_t\) is rent. \(s\) is the stock return and \(d\) is the drag, so \(r_s = (1+s-d)^{1/12}-1\).

\[ H_t = H_0 (1+g)^{t/12} \]
\[ S_0 = D + c_b H_0 \]
\[ S_t = S_{t-1}(1+r_s) + (O_t - R_t) \]
\[ T_0 = 0,\quad T_t = T_{t-1}(1+r_s) + \tau_t \]

\(S\) is the renter's portfolio. \(T\) is the owner's tax savings, invested at the same return. Because those savings sit in \(T\), they are not also subtracted from the renter's deposit.

If you sold that month

\[ \mathrm{Gain}_t = \max\left(0,\ H_t(1-c_s) - H_0(1+c_b)\right) \]
\[ G_t = \text{tax on }\max(0,\ \mathrm{Gain}_t - X_t)\text{, stacked on top of household income} \]

\(X_t\) is 0 until the home has been held 24 months, then $250,000 for a single filer and $500,000 for a joint return. Standard deduction the wages did not use comes off the taxable gain. Before 12 months the federal piece is ordinary income, stacked on the wages. After 12 months it is long-term gain, 0%, 15%, and 20% in the 2026 bands, and the wages fill those bands from the bottom. The 3.8% net investment income tax applies to the smaller of the taxable gain and the amount by which wages plus that gain exceed $250,000 joint or $200,000 single. State tax is the extra state tax from adding the gain on top of wages. Maryland adds 2% of the taxable gain when wages plus the gain exceed $350,000. Minnesota adds 1% of the gain above $1,000,000.

\[ L_t = H_t(1-c_s) - B_t - G_t \]
\[ W^H_t = \frac{L_t + T_t}{(1+i)^{t/12}},\qquad W^R_t = \frac{S_t}{(1+i)^{t/12}} \]

\(i\) is inflation. \(W^H\) is the buy line and \(W^R\) is the rent line, both in today's dollars. \(L_t\) is what's left after the loan, the sale, and tax on the gain.

Tax saved

Interest is deductible on the portion of the balance under the $750,000 acquisition-debt cap. At the start of month \(t\):

\[ \text{Deductible interest}_t = \text{Interest}_t \times \min\left(1,\ \frac{750{,}000}{B_{t-1}}\right) \]

Property tax and state income tax share the SALT cap. In 2026 that cap is $40,400. It rises 1% a year through 2029 and returns to $10,000 in 2030. Above $505,000 of income, growing the same way, the cap shrinks by 30 cents per dollar and does not fall below $10,000. The federal benefit is the tax on the renter's taxable income minus the tax on the owner's:

\[ \tau^{\mathrm{fed}} = \mathrm{Tax}(TI_{\mathrm{rent}}) - \mathrm{Tax}(TI_{\mathrm{own}}) \]

\(TI_{\mathrm{rent}}\) is wages minus the larger of the standard deduction and the deductions already on the return: state and local income tax, limited by that year's SALT cap, plus other itemized deductions. \(TI_{\mathrm{own}}\) also subtracts deductible mortgage interest and the property tax that still fits in the cap. \(\mathrm{Tax}\) is the 2026 ordinary schedule, so a deduction that crosses a bracket is split across the rates it passes. Federal savings stay $0 until housing deductions push itemizing past the standard deduction. State savings start with the first dollar of deductible interest. Property tax is not deducted on the state return here.

Property tax over time

\(\rho\) is the rate you entered, applied to the purchase price. California mode grows that assessment with inflation, and never by more than 2% a year. Market mode follows appreciation, so the effective rate stays constant. Inflation mode follows inflation and ignores appreciation. Year 0 is the purchase year, before any of that growth.

\[ \mathrm{Tax}^{\mathrm{CA}}_y = H_0 \,(1+\min(i,\ 0.02))^y \,\rho \]
\[ \mathrm{Tax}^{\mathrm{market}}_y = H_0 \,(1+g)^y \,\rho \]
\[ \mathrm{Tax}^{\mathrm{inflation}}_y = H_0 \,(1+i)^y \,\rho \]

Where the payment formula comes from

Each month the balance grows by \(r\), then the payment is subtracted. After \(t\) months the unpaid balance is the loan grown forward, minus the future value of the payments already made:

\[ B_t = P(1+r)^t - M \sum_{k=0}^{t-1} (1+r)^k \]

The sum is a geometric series, \(\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}(1+r)^k = ((1+r)^t - 1)/r\). The loan is paid off when \(B_N = 0\). Solving that for \(M\) gives the payment formula above. That formula is what the small chart draws, on a $1,000 loan. The comparison itself does not add unpaid interest to the balance.

Left out on purpose

  • Extra principal payments, refinancing, and adjustable rates.
  • Deducting PMI, and FHA loans that keep a premium for the life of the loan.
  • Income and the 2026 brackets are not inflated, so the marginal rate does not drift. A raise is not modeled.
  • Dependents, the alternative minimum tax, and New York's tax-benefit recapture.
  • Local income tax is a statewide average of the wage tax, except New York City, which uses its own brackets, and Philadelphia, which uses the resident wage tax. Those wage taxes are not applied to the home sale.
  • Payroll taxes, including California disability insurance. Alabama, Missouri, and Oregon let filers deduct some federal income tax; that deduction is not included.
  • A partial home-sale exclusion, depreciation from a rental period, and improvements added to basis.
  • Lumpy repairs, special assessments, and the chance that returns bounce around instead of arriving in a straight line.
