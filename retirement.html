How the lines are counted

280 simulated lives. Each year is a real S&P 500 year, dividends included, from 1928 through 2025, kept in 5-year stretches. Returns are Damodaran's (NYU Stern). Inflation is the BLS CPI-U from December to December, so each year's inflation covers the same months as its return.

Today's dollars

\(n\) is the stock return you type and \(\pi\) is inflation. The target real return is

\[ r = \frac{1+n}{1+\pi} - 1 \]

\(\tilde r\) is that historical year's real total return and \(\bar r\) is the average of those years. The block is shifted so the mean matches what you typed, then clamped:

\[ r_y = \mathrm{clamp}\!\left(\tilde r_{b(y)} + (r - \bar r),\; -0.7,\; 1.2\right) \]

\(r\) is the average year, the arithmetic mean. Wealth multiplies the years together, so it grows at the geometric mean instead, about half the variance lower. With these years' 19% volatility that is roughly two points a year:

\[ r_{\text{compound}} = \exp\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{98}\textstyle\sum_y \ln(1+r_y)\Big) - 1 \;\approx\; r - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2} \]

A straight 9% every year would be the optimistic version of this chart. The same average, with 1931 and 1974 and 2008 still in the deck, leaves a lower balance. Drawing a return each year instead of each month changes a lifetime very little next to whether a crash lands early in retirement.

While you work

\(S_0\) is salary today, \(g\) is the raise above inflation, and \(p\) is the fraction of pay set aside before tax. This is a 401(k), not an IRA. The 2026 IRA limit is $7,500, and that is not the cap here. \(\mathrm{cap}(a)\) is the 2026 workplace limit at that age, held flat in today's dollars. Elective deferrals are $24,500 and follow the pre-tax or Roth button. From 50 the catch-up adds $8,000, or $11,250 at ages 60 through 63. Pay over $150,000 makes that catch-up Roth even if the button says pre-tax. Mega backdoor fills what is left of the $72,000 annual-additions limit with Roth money, and it assumes the employer contributes nothing. The deposit cannot exceed that year's pay.

A pre-tax dollar of pay goes in whole. A Roth dollar is taxed first, so a dollar of pay buys \(1-t\) of Roth, where \(t\) is the federal plus California marginal rate on that year's wages. That keeps the two buttons fair. Roth and pre-tax come out equal when \(t\) is the same now and in retirement, because \(X(1-t_0)(1+r)^n = X(1+r)^n(1-t_1)\) when \(t_0 = t_1\). Without the \(1-t\), Roth at the same percent would quietly save more. The pay set aside fills each bucket in order: elective deferral, catch-up, then mega backdoor.

\[ C_y = \min\!\left(\text{pre-tax pay} + (1-t)\,\text{Roth pay},\; \mathrm{cap}(a)\right),\quad \text{pay used} \le S_0(1+g)^y \, p \]

That total is then split into the pre-tax and Roth pockets. A full working year adds the contribution at mid-year:

\[ B_{y+1} = B_y(1+r_y) + C_y\!\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{2} r_y\right) \]

After you retire

The spending knob is what you keep, not what leaves the account. \(K\) is that after-tax amount. \(SS\) is Social Security once it has started. \(W\) is the withdrawal that still leaves \(K\), and only the pre-tax share of \(W\) is ordinary income:

\[ W + SS - \mathrm{Tax}(f W, SS) = K \]

\(f\) is the pre-tax share of the balance that year. Withdrawals come out of the pre-tax and Roth pockets in proportion. A Roth withdrawal is left out of income, which assumes you are \(59\tfrac{1}{2}\) and the account is five years old. California does not tax Social Security. Up to 85% of the benefit can be taxed federally. The $25,000 and $34,000 thresholds ($32,000 and $44,000 married) are fixed in law and never indexed, so in today's dollars they shrink with inflation every year, and more of the benefit is taxed the later you claim. Brackets are tax year 2026 and stay put, because the chart is already in today's dollars. The federal standard deduction is $16,100 single or $32,200 married, plus $2,050 single or $1,650 per spouse married from the year you turn 65. The extra $6,000 senior deduction ends after 2028, so it is left out. California uses a $5,900 or $11,800 standard deduction, the exemption credit, and another $158 per person from the year you turn 65. Those credits phase out at high income and do not offset the 1% mental-health tax above $1 million of taxable income. Married means both spouses are the age on the slider. No payroll tax on the withdrawal, no Medicare premium surcharge, and no other income. Required minimum distributions are not forced.

A full retired year takes the withdrawal at mid-year. \(U\) is Social Security not needed that year. It is added to the Roth pocket, so it is not taxed a second time:

\[ \begin{gathered} B_{y+1} = \max\!\left(0,\; B_y - \tfrac{1}{2} W\right)(1+r_y) \\ -\; \tfrac{1}{2} W + U \end{gathered} \]

The lines

The lines are not rates, and none is one person's life. At each age they are a cutoff across the 280 balances: the level 9 in 10 histories beat, and the level 3 in 4 beat. The spending you set is checked against all 280: the verdict counts how many never run out, and the fix is the retirement age or save rate that gets that count to 252, 9 in 10. The burn rate is the largest \(K\), to the nearest $100, that never hits zero in at least that fraction of markets. Your slider is a separate plan. If a line reaches the floor, that fraction of markets had already run out.

The median is noted in the cards for fun, not drawn, and not to plan on. Spending it supports runs out in half of markets, and it isn't the average either: wealth multiplies, so a few lucky markets pull the average above where most end. Plan from the bad outcomes you would actually live through. The 9-in-10 number is the one Fidelity's planner uses.

The draws follow the historical success-rate method in Bengen (1994) and the Trinity study, and the block bootstrap used in later work: real multi-year stretches, not a bell curve. Those papers also mark the limit of this page. It uses only US stocks from 1928. Once other countries are included, the spending that survives is lower. The 5-year block length is a choice, not a law.

Left out: fees, an employer match, bonds, required minimum distributions, any pension besides the Social Security number, and the chance that future stocks do worse than 1928–2025 even after you lower the average. Not a quote, and not tax or investment advice.
