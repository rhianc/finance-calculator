How the lines are counted

Once a month. Savings go in at the end of a working month. In retirement, the withdrawal comes out at the start of the month, then what's left grows. Social Security starts at the later of the claim age and retirement, and it is already in today's dollars.

Real return = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1.

The monthly rate is that real return, compounded. A contribution that rises with inflation is a flat amount in today's dollars, so the savings input is added as-is. Same for the after-tax spending and for Social Security.

The knob is not what leaves the account. It is what you keep. Each retired year, the model looks for the withdrawal that still leaves that amount after federal tax, California tax, and the federal tax on Social Security. A traditional IRA withdrawal is all ordinary income. A Roth withdrawal is left out of income, which assumes you are 59½ and the account is five years old. California does not tax Social Security. Up to 85% of the benefit can be taxed federally.

Brackets are tax year 2026 and stay put, because the chart is already in today's dollars. The federal standard deduction is $16,100 single or $32,200 married, plus $2,050 or $1,650 from the year you turn 65. The extra $6,000 senior deduction ends after 2028, so it is left out. California uses a $5,900 or $11,800 standard deduction and the exemption credit. Married means one spouse, the age on the slider. No payroll tax on the withdrawal, no Medicare premium surcharge, and no other income.

The burn rate is a search: the largest after-tax spending level, to the nearest $100, whose balance never hits zero before the last month. Your slider is a separate plan drawn on the chart. If a line reaches the floor, that plan has run out, and the age is the first month it could not pay the withdrawal.

Left out: fees, a bad sequence of years, and any pension besides the Social Security number. Not a quote, and not tax or investment advice.
