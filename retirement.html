How the lines are counted

280 simulated lives. Each year is a real S&P 500 year, dividends included, from 1928 through 2025, kept in 5-year stretches.

Today's dollars

\(n\) is the stock return you type and \(\pi\) is inflation. The target real return is

\[ r = \frac{1+n}{1+\pi} - 1 \]

\(\tilde r\) is that historical year's real total return and \(\bar r\) is the average of those years. The block is shifted so the mean matches what you typed, then clamped:

\[ r_y = \mathrm{clamp}\!\left(\tilde r_{b(y)} + (r - \bar r),\; -0.7,\; 1.2\right) \]

A straight 10% every year was the optimistic version of this chart. The same average, with 1931 and 1974 and 2008 still in the deck, leaves a lower balance. Fidelity's planner draws a new return each year, not each month. Month versus year changes a lifetime very little next to whether a crash lands early in retirement.

While you work

\(S_0\) is salary today, \(g\) is the raise above inflation, and \(p\) is the fraction of salary saved. This is a 401(k), not an IRA. The 2026 IRA limit is $7,500, and that is not the cap here. \(\mathrm{cap}(a)\) is the 2026 workplace limit at that age, held flat in today's dollars. Elective deferrals are $24,500 and follow the pre-tax or Roth button. From 50 the catch-up adds $8,000, or $11,250 at ages 60 through 63. Pay over $150,000 makes that catch-up Roth even if the button says pre-tax. Mega backdoor fills what is left of the $72,000 annual-additions limit with Roth money, and it assumes the employer contributes nothing. The deposit cannot exceed that year's pay.

\[ C_y = \min\!\left(S_0(1+g)^y \, p,\; \mathrm{cap}(a),\; S_0(1+g)^y\right) \]

That total is then split into the pre-tax and Roth pockets. A full working year adds the contribution at mid-year:

\[ B_{y+1} = B_y(1+r_y) + C_y\!\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{2} r_y\right) \]

After you retire

The spending knob is what you keep, not what leaves the account. \(K\) is that after-tax amount. \(SS\) is Social Security once it has started. \(W\) is the withdrawal that still leaves \(K\), and only the pre-tax share of \(W\) is ordinary income:

\[ W + SS - \mathrm{Tax}(f W, SS) = K \]

\(f\) is the pre-tax share of the balance that year. Withdrawals come out of the pre-tax and Roth pockets in proportion. A Roth withdrawal is left out of income, which assumes you are \(59\tfrac{1}{2}\) and the account is five years old. California does not tax Social Security. Up to 85% of the benefit can be taxed federally. Brackets are tax year 2026 and stay put, because the chart is already in today's dollars. The federal standard deduction is $16,100 single or $32,200 married, plus $2,050 or $1,650 from the year you turn 65. The extra $6,000 senior deduction ends after 2028, so it is left out. California uses a $5,900 or $11,800 standard deduction, the exemption credit, and another $158 from the year you turn 65. Those credits phase out at high income and do not offset the 1% mental-health tax above $1 million of taxable income. Married means one spouse, the age on the slider. No payroll tax on the withdrawal, no Medicare premium surcharge, and no other income. Required minimum distributions are not forced.

A full retired year takes the withdrawal at mid-year. \(U\) is Social Security not needed that year. It is added to the Roth pocket, so it is not taxed a second time:

\[ \begin{gathered} B_{y+1} = \max\!\left(0,\; B_y - \tfrac{1}{2} W\right)(1+r_y) \\ -\; \tfrac{1}{2} W + U \end{gathered} \]

The three lines

The lines are not three rates, and none of them is one person's life. At each age they are a cutoff across the 280 balances: the level half the histories beat, the level 3 in 4 beat, and the level 9 in 10 beat. The burn rate is the largest \(K\), to the nearest $100, that never hits zero in at least that fraction of markets. Your slider is a separate plan. If a line reaches the floor, that fraction of markets had already run out.

The draws follow the historical success-rate method in Bengen (1994) and the Trinity study, and the block bootstrap used in later work: real multi-year stretches, not a bell curve. Those papers also mark the limit of this page. It uses only US stocks from 1928. Once other countries are included, the spending that survives is lower. The 5-year block length is a choice, not a law.

Left out: fees, an employer match, bonds, required minimum distributions, any pension besides the Social Security number, and the chance that future stocks do worse than 1928–2025 even after you lower the average. Not a quote, and not tax or investment advice.
